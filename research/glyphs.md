# Glyphs

What the scanner draws for every one of the 256 character codes.

The screen text returned by `STS` and `GST` is not all text. The radio's font
keeps pictures at the codes below 0x20 and above 0x7E, and the display carries
them in a line like any other character. A scanning screen always has two of
them on it, so anything drawing the screen rather than merely reading it has to
know what all 256 codes look like.

Everything here comes from Uniden's Font Data Specification, SDS200 (UB384Z),
version 1.00, issued 2018-08-02, which draws all 256 codes as pixel grids. The
remote command specification points at that document under `STS` (*"See "Font
Data Specification" for not ascii character code."*) and then leaves you there:
it is published nowhere, not on Uniden's site, not linked from any firmware or
support page, and not referenced by the SDS series specification at all. The
other place the font lives is the firmware, and that is encrypted. See
[oddities.md](oddities.md).

It is the SDS200's document, and this radio is an SDS150. Every code on a live
SDS150 screen lands where the document says it should, which is evidence that
the two share a font rather than proof of it.

---

## The two fonts

The document holds two tables, each covering 0x00 to 0xFF:

| Font | Cell | Where it is used |
| ---- | ---- | ---------------- |
| Small | 16 x 16 | Everything by default. 30 characters to a line |
| Large | 16 x 32 | The system, department and channel names. 24 characters to a line |

Twice the height is what the manual means by a large row occupying two lines in
the simple layouts, and `DSP_FORM` in `STS` is what says which font drew a given
line.

The pictures below are the small font, and they are the whole font. The large
table is this same set of shapes with every row drawn twice, which was measured
rather than assumed: glyphs pulled out of the large pages agree with the small
ones doubled to between 97 and 100%, `E` and `F` exactly, and every remaining
pixel is explained by the sampling of the reader. A renderer needs one table and
a way to draw it at double height, and nothing else.

Both tables draw a glyph 16 pixels wide, but the radio gives a large character a
20 pixel cell and stretches the picture across it, so 24 large characters fill
the same 480 pixels as 30 normal ones. Somebody did that arithmetic on purpose.
The panel is 480 by 320.

This file is also the font itself rather than a companion to one kept elsewhere.
The grids below are written so a person can read them and a program can parse
them, which means a renderer works from this copy instead of a packed one that
could drift from it. Change a glyph here and every drawing of it changes.

## How to read them

`#` is a lit pixel and `.` is not. Every grid is 16 wide and 16 tall.

Several pictures are halves of one icon, drawn in consecutive cells because a 16
pixel cell is too narrow to hold them, and neither half means anything alone.
Where the specification gives a run of codes one name, they are numbered below
as *part 1 of 2*, *part 2 of 2*, and so on, left to right.

Two are on screen whenever the scanner is scanning: `0x1A` and `0x1B`, in the
two cells at the end of line 1, which the screen map calls `Direction` and the
manual labels `DIR`. They are the two halves of one arrow, and the field shows
which way the scan is running. `0x1C` and `0x1D` are the same arrangement
pointing down.

`0x17` to `0x19` are the three pieces of the volume and squelch bar, drawn end,
middle, end, so a bar of any length is built from them.

Codes the specification leaves empty are listed too, with a note instead of a
grid, so a reader can tell "nothing is drawn here" apart from "this file does
not say." Absence you have verified is data. Absence you have not is a hole.

## Where these came from

The specification is a drawing rather than a font file: each glyph is a grid of
filled and empty cells on a page, and the only way to get the bitmaps out is to
read the picture back. Whoever drew the document made that possible, deliberately
or not, by rendering everything in three exact greys, one for the table's own
borders, one for the ruling inside a grid and one for a filled pixel. The lattice
can therefore be found by colour rather than guessed at from a pitch. A filled
cell paints over the ruling at its own edge, so the grids are placed from the
borders that fence them, which no glyph can erase. That three-grey discipline is
the kind of quiet craftsmanship nobody ever gets thanked for.

Two checks say the reading is right, and both cover all 256 codes rather than a
sample. Every code the specification names is drawn, and every code it leaves
unnamed is blank, with no disagreement anywhere in the table. And every
printable ASCII glyph is legible as the character it should be, with its bottom
row clear, which is where a grid off by a single cell shows up first. Transcribe
by machine and you do not spot-check; you check everything, and you aim the
checks at the failure you would actually expect.

matter most: the modulation tags, the function-key indicators, the degree sign,
the volume and squelch bar in three pieces, and the direction arrow that is on
screen the whole time the radio is scanning.

## A note on the WiFi icons

This radio has no WiFi, the `WiFi` field the specification gives `Property` is
absent from it entirely, and yet the font carries four WiFi icons at `0xC0` to
`0xC7`. They belong to a sibling model, carried along because the platform
shares one font across the family. A font is a fossil record that way: long
after the product lines diverge, the shapes stay in the table, waiting for a
screen that will never call them.
